Structured Study Notes: Why 95% of AI Agents Fail in Production (And How to Fix It)
The Hard Problem of Building AI Agents
	• The hardest part isn't building the agent (building it is the fun part).
	• The hard part is knowing whether what you built is actually any good.
	• Running an AI agent once, giving it tools, and seeing it do something magical is not the same as it actually working in production.
	• That gap—the little voice asking "but does it actually work?"—has a name: Evaluation or Evals for short.
	• Evals are one of the most critical concepts to master to deploy AI agents into production.

What is an AI Eval?
	• An AI Eval is a structured way of measuring how good your AI outputs really are.
	• Instead of eyeballing a couple of answers and saying "that looks fine," you set up a repeatable process that gives you an actual score.
	• That score answers one core question: Is my AI agent getting better, or did I do something to break it?
	• Every time you change a prompt, swap a model, or tweak your retrieval, an eval tells you whether you helped the agent or silently broke something.
Benchmarks vs. AI Evals
	• Benchmarks (e.g., SWE-bench for coding, TAU-bench for customer service, Terminal Bench for command-line tasks, Agentic Analysis Index):
		○ Test the underlying model using a generic test applied across every model.
		○ Great for deciding which model to start with.
		○ A model sitting at the top of a leaderboard can still completely fall apart on your specific use case because the benchmark never saw your data, your prompt, or your users.
	• AI Evals:
		○ Test your specific agentic system on your task, with your data.
		○ How you determine if the agent actually works for your specific application.
Why Evals Matter Specifically for AI Agents
	• An agent isn't just a single answer; it's a whole chain of decisions (picking a tool, calling it, reading results, deciding the next action).
	• There are many places where things can quietly go wrong (quietly go sideways).
	• When an AI agent breaks in production, you need to know where and why. Reading transcripts one at a time at 2:00 AM won't scale.
	• Research shows roughly a one-third gap between how models score in the lab and how they perform in real deployed work. Evals close that gap before users feel it.
Traditional Machine Learning Evals vs. Modern AI Evals
	• Traditional Machine Learning Evals:
		○ Clean, discrete outputs: categories (spam vs. not spam), numbers (predicted house prices), or simple yes/no.
		○ Grading was clean and objective using a test set with ground-truth answers.
		○ Metrics used: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score.
		○ Analogy: Grading a multiple-choice test (there is a clear answer key; just count correct answers).
	• LLM & AI Agent Evals:
		○ Outputs are open-ended text or a sequence of actions; there isn't just one single correct answer.
		○ Example: For an article summary, there could be 100 good summaries and 100 bad ones, and two great summaries can look completely different.
		○ Analogy: Grading an essay (there is no single right answer; you need a rubric and judgment).
		○ AI evals are inherently messier and more qualitative.
Core Metrics and Matching Them to Tasks
Top 4 Core Metrics:
	1. Relevance: Did the output actually answer what was asked?
	2. Faithfulness / Groundedness: Is the response true and backed by real data, or is the model making things up? (Hallucination check).
	3. Correctness: Did it match the expected answer when there is one?
	4. Coherence: Does it read well?
Matching Metrics to Specific Tasks:
	• Summarization: Care about Faithfulness (true to source without inventing stuff), Coverage (capturing main points), and Conciseness (keeping it tight).
	• Classification: Uses traditional ML metrics like Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 Score (since outputs are clean labels).
	• Translation: Care about meaning preservation and natural reading flow in the target language. Uses metrics like BLEU and COMET.
	• Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): Uses Faithfulness and Answer Relevance to pinpoint whether failure was caused by bad retrieval or bad generation.
The Three Buckets of AI Evaluation Metrics
	1. Classic Overlap Metrics:
		○ Examples: BLEU (translation), ROUGE (summarization), METEOR.
		○ Mechanics: Measures exact word overlap between output and reference answer.
		○ Pros/Cons: Fast, cheap, simple, but completely blind to meaning (two sentences saying the exact same thing in different words get different scores).
	2. Semantic Metrics:
		○ Examples: BERTScore, BLEURT, COMET.
		○ Mechanics: Compares meanings using embeddings.
		○ Pros/Cons: Handles paraphrasing significantly better than word overlap metrics.
	3. Model-Based Metrics:
		○ Examples: LLM-as-a-Judge, G-Eval.
		○ Mechanics: Uses a strong LLM to grade outputs against a rubric.
		○ Pros/Cons: Flexible enough to grade open-ended, creative outputs with no reference answers.
The Golden Rule: Start with a Golden Dataset
	• Do NOT start by picking metrics off a list.
	• Start by building a Golden Dataset:
		○ A collection of example inputs paired with what a great output looks like and what a bad output looks like.
		○ Includes common cases, annoying edge cases, and every past failure seen in production.
		○ Scale: Start small with 5 to 20 examples and grow it over time.
	• Working Backwards:
		1. Build the Golden Dataset first.
		2. Look at the examples and ask: "What makes this output good, and what makes it bad?"
		3. Quantify those criteria—those become your Metrics.
The Four Ways to Execute Grading (Graders)
	1. Human Evals:
		○ Domain experts sit down and grade outputs against a rubric.
		○ Gold standard for quality, but slow, expensive, and non-scalable.
		○ Best used for small, careful sampling as the ground truth.
	2. User Evals:
		○ Direct implicit/explicit feedback from real users in live production (thumbs up/down, accept/reject, edits made, task completion, repeat queries).
		○ Ultimate real-world ground truth, but can be noisy and only available after deployment (cannot serve as a pre-deployment safety net).
	3. Code-Based / Programmatic Evals:
		○ Simple checks written in code (checking exact value matches, valid JSON formatting, correct tool call arguments, execution latency, cost).
		○ Cheap, instant, and scalable to run automatically on every change, but only works for clearly checkable answers (pairs perfectly with classification).
	4. LLM-as-a-Judge:
		○ Using a strong, large model to grade outputs against your rubric.
		○ Scales essay-style grading across thousands of open-ended outputs (e.g., evaluating helpfulness or groundedness).
		○ Critical Rule: Judge models have biases (preferring longer answers or favoring the first response shown). Always validate LLM judge grades against human-graded samples before trusting them at scale.
The Continuous Evaluation Loop (CI/CD for AI)
Evals are not a one-time report card before launch; they are a continuous loop analogous to automated software testing.

                  │ 1. Define "Good" Specially    │
                  └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                 ▼
                  ┌──────────────────────────────┐

                  └──────────────┬───────────────┘

                                 ▼

                  │ 4. Establish Baseline Score  │
                  └──────────────┬───────────────┘

                  └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                 ▼

                  └──────────────┬───────────────┘

                  │ 8. Monitor Production & Loop │
                  └──────────────────────────────┘

	• Baseline: Running the current system across the full dataset to get a starting score. Without a baseline, "it feels better" is just vibe eval.
	• Failure Grouping: Categorizing failures (e.g., retrieval misses, formatting errors, weird edge cases) pinpoints exactly what to fix.
	• Regression Prevention: Re-running evals after adjustments ensures improvements were made without breaking previously working features.
	• Production Monitoring: Model providers may silently update underlying models overnight, changing behavior without code changes. Production failures must feed back into the Golden Dataset.
Ecosystem Tools for Evals and Monitoring
	• Eval Execution Tools:
		○ Promptfoo: Test prompts and run evals directly from the command line.
		○ Ragas: Library providing out-of-the-box RAG evaluation metrics.
	• Tracing & Production Monitoring Tools:
		○ LangFuse

You will never ever know if your AI Agent actually works by watching it succeed one time. 


LLM Fine-Tuning Explained: The Complete Guide
Pre-Training vs. Post-Training
	• Pre-Training:
		○ Carried out internally by foundational labs (e.g., OpenAI training GPT-5.5, Anthropic training Claude Opus 4.7, Meta training Llama models, Google training Gemini models).
		○ Runs massive training jobs on huge GPU clusters using trillions of tokens of data.
		○ Produces the base model (the out-of-the-box pre-trained model available on platforms like Hugging Face or via APIs).
	• Post-Training:
		○ Encompasses everything that happens after pre-training.
		○ Fine-Tuning is the most common form of post-training.
	• Evolution from Traditional ML to LLMs:
		○ Traditional Machine Learning: Teams built and trained models from scratch on their own data, then fine-tuned those same models on specific data for specialized tasks (owning the entire pipeline).
		○ LLM Era (Model as a Service): Foundational labs create the pre-trained model and serve it via API. Developers adapt this pre-trained model to perform better on specific use cases rather than training from zero.
	• Parameter Scale Challenge:
		○ Traditional ML: Parameters ranged from hundreds of thousands to a few million.
		○ LLMs: Parameters range from a few billion (small open-source models) to 10–70 billion (production standards), hundreds of billions (frontier models), and over 1 trillion parameters (models like GLM).
		○ Core Challenge: Updating even a fraction of a 1-trillion-parameter model cannot be casually done on a single GPU.
Open-Weight vs. Closed-Source Models
	• Open-Weight Models:
		○ Hosted by inference providers (e.g., Hugging Face Inference, Nebius Token Factory) distributing weights across GPUs.
		○ Examples: Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM.
		○ Fine-Tuning Capability: Because model weights are exposed and accessible, you can directly modify parameters, fine-tune, and deploy on your own infrastructure.
	• Closed-Source Models:
		○ Examples: Anthropic (Claude), OpenAI (GPT series), Google (Gemini).
		○ Fine-Tuning Capability: You cannot fine-tune them on private infrastructure because you do not have direct access to their weights.
		○ Fine-tuning must be done through native fine-tuning APIs/services (e.g., OpenAI Fine-Tuning API, Google Vertex AI, Anthropic Fine-Tuning Service).
		○ Trade-off: You submit data, they execute training on their infrastructure, and you call the fine-tuned model via API without visibility into under-the-hood operations.
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) vs. Full Fine-Tuning
	• PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning):
		○ Core Concept: Instead of updating every parameter in a massive model (e.g., 70B parameters), PEFT modifies only a tiny fraction of parameters required for a task.
		○ LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation):
			§ Freezes the original base model weights.
			§ Injects small, trainable matrices into specific layers of the model.
			§ Only trains those small matrices. At inference time, they are combined with frozen weights to produce adapted behavior.
			§ Impact: Reduces trainable parameters from ~70 Billion down to ~100 Million, drastically cutting compute, memory, and cost.
		○ QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation):
			§ Takes LoRA a step further by quantizing the frozen base model down to 4-bit precision.
			§ Runs LoRA adapters on top of the 4-bit quantized base.
			§ Enables fine-tuning a 70 Billion parameter model on a single H100 GPU.
			§ Serves as the default starting point for teams fine-tuning open-source models using libraries like Hugging Face's peft.
	• Full Fine-Tuning:
		○ Updates every single parameter in the model.
		○ Offers maximum flexibility and quality, but is extremely expensive, slow, and requires heavy GPU infrastructure.
		○ Used only as a fallback when PEFT fails to hit the required quality bar.
Reinforcement Fine-Tuning & Alignment Methods
	1. Reinforcement Fine-Tuning with Verifiable Rewards:
		○ Technique powering reasoning models (e.g., OpenAI 'o' series, DeepSeek-R1).
		○ Applied to tasks where answers are automatically verifiable (e.g., math problems or code that can execute tests).
		○ The model generates multiple attempts: correct attempts receive rewards, wrong attempts get penalized.
		○ Learns to reason over time without needing human labelers.
	2. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback):
		○ The classic alignment technique behind systems like ChatGPT.
		○ Trains a separate Reward Model based on human preference data.
		○ Uses algorithms like PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) to steer the primary language model using the Reward Model.
		○ Highly effective, but expensive and complex due to human labeling requirements and training loop complexity.
	3. Preference Optimization (DPO - Direct Preference Optimization):
		○ A simpler alternative to RLHF that skips the separate reward model completely.
		○ Directly optimizes the language model on preference pairs in a single training step.
		○ Lower compute requirements, easier implementation; default choice for open-source preference tuning.
Decision Framework: Choosing the Right Strategy
	1. Phase 1 (Pre-Fine-Tuning Baseline):
		○ Optimize Prompts first.
		○ Optimize Context / RAG second.
		○ Establishes a performance benchmark to measure actual uplift gained from fine-tuning.
	2. Phase 2 (Fine-Tuning Methodology Selection):
		○ Domain Adaptation / Task Precision / Tone: Start with QLoRA.
		○ High-Quality Fallback: Move to Full Fine-Tuning only if QLoRA/LoRA performance is insufficient.
		○ Human Preference / Alignment (Safety, Style, Tone): Use DPO or RLHF.
		○ Reasoning / Verifiable Tasks (Code, Math): Use Reinforcement Fine-Tuning with Verifiable Rewards.
Critical Challenges in Fine-Tuning
	• Data Quality: Fine-tuning a good base model with a poor-quality dataset degrades performance rather than improving it.
	• Evaluation Framework: A clear evaluation dataset (Eval Set) and precise metrics must be established before fine-tuning to verify if training brought measurable improvement.

Prompt Engineering is Dead. The Future is Loop Engineering.
The Evolution of AI Paradigms
	• Era 1: Prompt Engineering
		○ The highest leverage skill was crafting the exact wording of the prompt.
		○ Phrasing changed output quality because models were rigid and input words carried all the weight.
	• Era 2: Context Engineering
		○ As models improved, the bottleneck shifted from phrasing to what the model sees at inference time.
		○ Focused on curating everything landing in the context window: retrieved documents, conversation history, memory, and tool results.
	• Era 3: Loop Engineering (The Current Paradigm - 2026)
		○ Models write their own prompts and pull their own context via tool calls, meaning inputs are no longer a scarce skill.
		○ The scarce skill is designing the loop the agent runs inside.
		○ Loop Engineering Defined: Designing the cycle of Act $\rightarrow$ Observe $\rightarrow$ Verify $\rightarrow$ Retry instead of handwriting every step manually.
		○ Origin: Came directly out of teams building coding agents (e.g., Corgi) and AI-native companies replacing floors of human agents.
Running Example: AI Customer Support Agent
	• Used throughout the breakdown to illustrate loop design: An AI support agent for an online store handling order questions, refunds, and shipping status.
Anatomy of the Agent Loop
	• The Core Loop Primitive (ReAct Pattern):
		1. The model gets called.
		2. The model decides on an action (almost always a tool call).
		3. The harness executes that action.
		4. Results return as an observation.
		5. The observation is appended to the context.
		6. The model gets called again (Reason, Act, Observe, Repeat).
	• Customer Support Loop Execution Flow:
		○ Iteration 1: Classifies intent as request refund.
		○ Iteration 2: Decides order details are needed $\rightarrow$ calls order lookup tool. Observation (order record) returns to context.
		○ Iteration 3: Checks refund against policy.
		○ Iteration 4: Drafts reply and either sends or escalates.
Stopping Conditions & Economics of Loops
	• The Stopping Condition Problem:
		○ Without explicit termination, agents experience runaway loops: repeatedly calling tools, re-reasoning, spinning on uncompletable tasks, and burning tokens.
	• Explicit Termination Mechanisms (Use multiple in production):
		○ Model emits a clear done signal or final answer.
		○ Hard cap on maximum iterations.
		○ Cost or token budget that kills the loop.
		○ A verifier passes and ends the loop.
	• Loop Economics & Performance Optimization:
		○ Every iteration is a full model call $\rightarrow$ latency and cost scale directly with loop length.
		○ Taking 10 steps for a 2-step task equals 10x the inference bill and 10x the failure surface area.
		○ Goal: Design the tightest loop that completes the task reliably.
Anti-Pattern: Point Solution Thinking
	• The Mistake: Treating agents as single-point solutions by obsessing over single node generation in isolation (e.g., hyper-tuning a reply writer or intent classifier).
	• The Reality: A support agent is a loop where every step feeds the next. If an intent classifier is flawless but the order lookup tool times out without handling, the system fails.
	• Optimizing a single node in an interdependent graph yields almost no system-level benefit.
Harness Engineering
	• Definition: All the deterministic software wrapped around the model.
		○ Model = ~5% of the magic.
		○ Harness = Determines whether the agent survives real production traffic.
	• Core Responsibilities of the Harness:
		○ Retry logic when a tool call fails.
		○ Timeouts when databases are slow.
		○ Fallbacks when the model returns malformed output.
		○ Guardrails stopping unauthorized actions (e.g., promising unauthorized refunds).
		○ Structured logging and tracing to reconstruct production failures.
	• System-Level Evaluation (Evals):
		○ Moving beyond single output quality ("Was this reply good?") to evaluating whole system behavior under stress.
		○ Evaluate handling of lookup failures, graceful degradation, hallucination prevention under error, angry customers, and out-of-policy refunds.
		○ Use tools like LangSmith or LangFuse to score end-to-end trace runs, not isolated completions.
Context Management & Context Rot
	• Context Rot Defined: As loops iterate, tool results, intermediate reasoning, and observations accumulate. Before hitting hard token limits, model quality degraded due to filling the window with stale, low-signal tokens.
	• Context Management Strategies:
		1. Compaction: Periodically summarize earlier steps into short-running summaries.
		2. External Memory: Write facts out to external storage; retrieve only relevant steps into line.
		3. Tool Result Management: Filter, extract, and summarize massive tool payloads (e.g., large database query responses) before inserting them into context.
	• Multi-Agent Orchestration as Context Strategy:
		○ Multi-agent architectures serve as a context management strategy, not just a capability flex.
		○ Spin off sub-agents with clean, scoped context for bounded tasks, returning only the final result to the main loop to keep the primary context lean.
		○ Context must be budgeted like cost and iterations.
Verification Strategies in the Loop
	• Asymmetry of Verification:
		○ Cheap / Automated Verifiers: Fast, deterministic checks. Put these inside the loop so the agent catches mistakes and retries automatically.
			§ Coding Example: Code compiles and passes unit tests.
			§ Support Example: Check drafted reply against deterministic policy rules (e.g., "Any refund over $100 must escalate").
		○ Expensive / Subjective Verifiers: Requires nuanced human judgment.
			§ Support Example: Judging whether a refund decision was generally "reasonable."
			§ Put humans in the loop where automated verification is unavailable.
The Mint Framework (Minimal Intelligence Necessary Tools)
	• Core Philosophy: Build the minimal viable system end-to-end before adding any complexity.
	• Incremental Construction Sequence:
		1. Basic Prompt + Basic Workflow: Message comes in $\rightarrow$ agent answers (end-to-end working).
		2. Tool Use: Add tool access (e.g., actual order lookup).
		3. Continuous Evaluation: Measure real resolution vs. silent failure.
		4. State & Memory: Retain customer context across conversations and tickets.
		5. More Workflows: Add dedicated sub-flows (refund flow, escalation flow).
		6. Human-in-the-Loop & Multi-Agent Orchestration: Add ONLY at the end when demonstrated real needs arise (for hard cases or context isolation).
	• System Observability: Incremental additions keep systems observable, allowing clear localization of failures during debugging.
	• Failure Questions for Every Layer:
		○ How does this break?
		○ What does the system do when it breaks? (50% of failure modes are traditional software issues like timeouts, null values, and API downtimes).
Andrew Ng’s Three Nested Loops of Software Construction
Software development with agents operates across three nested loops running at different time frequencies:
	1. Agent Decoding Loop (Seconds to Minutes):
		○ Operator: Run by the Agent.
		○ Mechanism: Given a specification, the agent writes code, tests its work, and iterates until specs are met.
	2. Developer Feedback Loop (Tens of Minutes to Hours):
		○ Operator: Run by the Developer.
		○ Mechanism: Developer reviews agent outputs, steers direction, updates specs, and feeds policy updates/corrections back.
	3. External Feedback Loop (Hours to Weeks):
		○ Operator: Run by the World (Real users, alpha testers, A/B tests, production data).
		○ Mechanism: Production feedback updates product vision and specifications, which trickle back into the inner loops.
	• Insight: Verification relies more on human judgment the further out the loop goes. Humans shift to where context and judgment advantages lie.
Mapping the 4 Agentic Design Patterns to the Loop
Andrew Ng’s four original agentic design patterns function as literal building blocks within the engineered loop:
	• Reflection: The agent checks its draft against policy (the in-loop verifier).
	• Tool Use: Querying external tools (order lookup).
	• Planning: Sequencing steps (Classify $\rightarrow$ Lookup $\rightarrow$ Decide $\rightarrow$ Respond).
	• Multi-Agent Collaboration: Sub-agents executing bounded sub-tasks and returning clean results.
Actionable System Design Checklist
	• Avoid point-solution thinking; design the whole loop.
	• Explicitly design stopping conditions and budget iterations, cost, and context.
	• Prioritize harness engineering and system-level tracing/evaluations equally with model evaluations.
	• Embed cheap verifiers inside the loop; position humans where verification is expensive.
	• Follow the Mint framework: start minimal end-to-end and scale complexity incrementally.
